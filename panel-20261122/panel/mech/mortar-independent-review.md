# Independent algorithm review: mortar clearance candidate

Reviewed source: `experiments/mech-mortar-arc/bot/lib/mortar.js` and the corresponding mortar block of `firecontrol.js`. This review is independent of the implementing Rust/physics reviewer. It is a static mathematical review; the 18 crafted test results in `mortar-arc-cases.json` belong to that reviewer, not to this reviewer's execution.

The shared `mortarSolve` computes a flight time after the forward barrel offset. The new helper uses that same convention: the launch point is the tube point plus `offset * direction`, and subsequent position is `launch + speed * direction * t - (0, 0, gravity * t² / 2)`. This repairs the old dimensional mismatch between a path-distance horizontal displacement and a horizontal-distance flight-time denominator. The near-tube segment is checked separately before the flight segments.

The returned flight and pitch are subject to positive finite speed/gravity/time/TTL checks, both live pitch bounds, and a maximum of 64 segments. For a constant-gravity parabola and chord interval h, the maximum vertical deviation is g h² / 8. The helper's time-resolution rule bounds that deviation to 0.08 m, and additional time and path-length limits tighten it for many shots. This is a bounded geometric approximation, not a proof of the server's finite projectile collision behavior. In particular, a very thin overhead object between a chord and the arc can be missed; a low obstacle can be conservatively rejected even when the curved trajectory clears it. The unchanged `world.shotClear` collision implementation and the server's uninspected contact model limit any stronger claim.

The new self-clearance check considers both the current feet position and its constant-velocity projection through release delay plus flight time. It includes body radius as well as splash radius and the existing margin. That is safer than the old current-position-only test, but it does not bound all future accelerations or establish the actual first-impact point if an unexpected actor intercepts the shell. Full-arc blocking catches the newly demonstrated distant wall that the old first-segment check missed.

The patch does not change pickup preferences, promote mortar acquisition, select a new high arc, copy opponent logic, or modify the firing branches for other weapons. The control flow still requires a permitted tactical target, readiness, a non-busy laser and actual torso settlement before firing. I found no blocking inconsistency in the offset/time algebra, finite-input handling, segment cap or integration order. I recommend retaining the patch as a separate candidate or explicit combined component, with conformance/timing receipts tied to the final integrated source. A score improvement remains unmeasured.
